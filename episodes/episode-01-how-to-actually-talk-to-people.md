# EPISODE 1 — HOW TO ACTUALLY TALK TO PEOPLE

**Hook:** Most people are better at conversation than they think, worse at ending one than they think, and have never been told the two-question rule that fixes almost everything.

**Speakers:** NICK (host), MIA (communication and psychology), SAM (sport and performance, joins mid-episode)

HOST: Alright. Let's make it practical, because we're at the point where I want a list of things I'd actually do this week. Mia, you first, then Sam. Short ones.

MIA: One. The second question. Whatever they say, ask one follow-up about it before you say anything about yourself. Just one. If you only take one thing from this episode, take that.

HOST: Two?

MIA: Answer with a doorknob. Never say "good" and stop. Give them one detail they can grab.

SAM: Three. Before you respond to anything that matters, name the conversation. Are they solving, feeling, or working out where they stand with you? Half a second. Then match it.

HOST: Four. The ask. "Do you want solutions or a rant buddy?" I'm going to use that one at home and report back.

MIA: Five. Leave with a reason and a compliment. Reason, compliment, go. You're allowed to leave. Everyone's bad at guessing when you want to anyway.

SAM: Six. In a disagreement, say the receptive bits out loud. "I get why you'd think that, I might be wrong, here's where I've landed." And ask what would change their mind. And say what would change yours.

MIA: Seven. Tell stories back-to-front. Start with the bit that makes them need to know, cut the setup, land it, hand the ball back.

HOST: And eight is mine, and it's the one from the top. Remember the liking gap. Whatever you think they thought of you, add two points. That's probably closer.

MIA: [amused] That's actually a decent summary of the whole field.

HOST: Right, rapid fire. Quick answers, no hedging, Mia, you've hedged enough for one episode.

MIA: [laughing] It's called being accurate.

HOST: Would you rather be stuck in a lift for an hour with someone who only asks questions, or someone who only tells stories?

MIA: Questions. At least I'm involved.

SAM: Stories. I've got nothing to say in a lift anyway.

HOST: Myth or fact. Eye contact should be held the whole time.

MIA: Myth. Constant eye contact reads as a threat or a sales pitch. Look at them when they're talking, look away sometimes when you are.

HOST: You're at a work function, you don't know anyone, and there's a group of four already talking. What's the actual move?

MIA: Stand near them, angle your body toward the group, wait for a laugh or a pause, and come in on the topic. "Sorry, are you talking about the new parking? Because I've got thoughts." Never introduce yourself first, join the topic first.

HOST: Sam, what's the best communicator you've seen in Australian sport?

SAM: I'm not naming one, but the pattern's always the same. The ones who can be blunt without being cruel, because everyone around them knows they've been watching properly.

HOST: Someone tells a story you've heard three times. Do you say something?

MIA: If it's a mate, "is this the one where he fell in the pool?" with a smile. If it's your nan, no. Never.

HOST: What's the one phrase that instantly kills a conversation?

SAM: "Anyway."

MIA: "That's interesting." It's what you say when it isn't.

HOST: [laughing] I say that constantly.

MIA: We know.

HOST: Would you rather have one deep conversation a week or ten small ones?

MIA: Ten small ones, and I'll defend that. The research on small interactions is stronger than people expect, and the deep ones tend to grow out of them anyway.

SAM: One deep one. But I'd want to pick the person.

HOST: Last one. Text or call, when you've stuffed up?

MIA: Call. Or in person. Apologies over text lose all the tone that makes them sound sincere, and you've just told me the words are only seven percent.

HOST: [pauses] I walked into that.

MIA: You did.

HOST: Okay. Wrapping up. If I had to hand someone this episode in one line, it's that conversation is a set of small mechanical moves that most of us never got taught, and the biggest obstacle is that we're all more scared of each other than we need to be. Catch the ball, throw it back with handles, ask the second question, match the conversation you're in, and get out with a compliment. That's ninety percent of it.

MIA: And the other ten percent is remembering that the other person's worried about exactly what you're worried about.

SAM: And that the switch from feeling to fixing should take two seconds, not two sets.

And specifically, I want to know why I'll drive across Brisbane to save fifteen dollars on a drill and then not check what my super's invested in for five years.

MIA: [laughing] Because you're a person.

## RESEARCH NOTES — not for audio

**Liking gap.** Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom & Clark (2018), "The Liking Gap in Conversations: Do People Like Us More Than We Think?", *Psychological Science*. Strangers, lab conversations, and a longitudinal roommate sample; people consistently underestimated how much partners liked them and enjoyed their company.

**Talking to strangers / mispredicting enjoyment.** Epley & Schroeder (2014), "Mistakenly Seeking Solitude", *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* — Chicago commuters (trains and buses). Replicated in London (Schroeder, Lyons & Epley, 2022, *JEP: General*). Barista study: Sandstrom & Dunn (2014), "Is Efficiency Overrated? Minimal Social Interactions Lead to Belonging and Positive Affect", *Social Psychological and Personality Science*. Deep vs shallow conversations: Kardas, Kumar & Epley (2022), "Overly Shallow? Miscalibrated Expectations Create a Barrier to Deeper Conversation", *JPSP*.

**Follow-up questions.** Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson & Gino (2017), "It Doesn't Hurt to Ask: Question-Asking Increases Liking", *JPSP*. Includes a speed-dating field dataset and experimental manipulation of question quantity. The follow-up-question effect on liking was the strongest of the question types examined. I described the controls conservatively in the script; check the paper if you want to quote effect sizes.

**Shift vs support response.** Derber (1979), *The Pursuit of Attention: Power and Ego in Everyday Life*. The "conversational narcissism" framing is his.

**Doorknobs / givers and takers.** Mastroianni (2022), "Good conversations have lots of doorknobs", Experimental History (Substack essay — not peer-reviewed, and presented in the script as an idea rather than a finding).

**Conversations ending.** Mastroianni, Gilbert, Cooney & Wilson (2021), "Do conversations end when people want them to?", *PNAS*. Both parties wanted the same ending point in only a very small fraction of conversations, and estimates of the partner's preferred end point were off by a large proportion of conversation length. The script deliberately avoids quoting exact percentages.

**Four-second silence.** Koudenburg, Postmes & Gordijn (2011), "Disrupting the flow: How brief silences in group conversations affect social needs", *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*. Dutch sample; four-second pauses reduced feelings of belonging and self-esteem.

**Three conversations / matching.** Duhigg (2024), *Supercommunicators* (Random House). Popular synthesis, not primary research; credited as such in the script.

**Four Horsemen / soft start-up.** Gottman & Silver (1999), *The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work*; underlying observational work from the Gottman "Love Lab" studies (e.g. Gottman & Levenson, 1992, *JPSP*). Predictive accuracy claims from this literature are sometimes overstated in popular coverage; the script keeps to "pretty good at predicting" and names contempt as the strongest single predictor, which is consistent with Gottman's own reporting.

**Conversational receptiveness.** Yeomans, Minson, Collins, Chen & Gino (2020), "Conversational receptiveness: Improving engagement with opposing views", *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*. The four linguistic markers (acknowledgement, hedging, positive framing, agreement) are drawn directly from the paper.

**Mehrabian myth.** Mehrabian & Wiener (1967) and Mehrabian & Ferris (1967); the 7-38-55 figures come from combining these two narrow experiments on single-word affective messages. Mehrabian has publicly stated the figures should not be generalised to communication in general.
